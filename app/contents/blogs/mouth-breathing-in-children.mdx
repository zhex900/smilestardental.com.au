**Mouth breathing in children is common during a cold or when the nose is blocked, but persistent mouth breathing during the day or while sleeping may indicate an underlying breathing or airway concern.** Possible causes include allergies, enlarged tonsils or adenoids, nasal congestion and certain oral habits. If your child regularly sleeps with their mouth open, snores or appears tired despite a full night's sleep, a professional assessment is recommended.

At Smile Star Dental, we understand that parents may notice small changes in how their children breathe, sleep or hold their mouths without knowing whether these signs are important.

Some breathing patterns are temporary, while others deserve closer attention.

In this guide, we'll explain the signs of mouth breathing in children, common causes, possible effects on sleep and dental development, and the treatment options that may be considered following an assessment.

## What Is Mouth Breathing in Children?

Mouth breathing occurs when a child regularly breathes through their mouth instead of their nose.

Children may breathe through their mouths occasionally when exercising, crying or experiencing a blocked nose.

However, persistent mouth breathing at rest or during sleep may warrant investigation.

Nasal breathing normally allows air to pass through structures that filter, warm and humidify it.

When nasal airflow is restricted, a child may rely more heavily on mouth breathing.

In some cases, the breathing pattern may continue even after the original obstruction has improved.

### Is it normal for children to breathe through their mouths?

Occasional mouth breathing is not necessarily a problem.

For example, a child with a cold may temporarily sleep with their mouth open because of nasal congestion.

However, regular mouth breathing when the child is otherwise well should not automatically be dismissed as a habit.

It may indicate an underlying nasal obstruction or another condition requiring assessment.

We recommend paying attention to how frequently it happens and whether other symptoms are present.

### What age should children start breathing through their noses?

Nasal breathing is normally an important part of breathing throughout childhood.

There is no particular age when persistent mouth breathing suddenly becomes concerning.

Instead, we consider the child's age, symptoms, medical history and whether nasal breathing is comfortable.

For example, a toddler with a temporary cold has a different clinical situation from a school-aged child who has been mouth breathing and snoring for several months.

### Is sleeping with an open mouth the same as mouth breathing?

Not always.

A child's lips may occasionally fall open during sleep without indicating significant nasal obstruction.

However, consistently sleeping with an open mouth, particularly alongside snoring, noisy breathing or disturbed sleep, deserves closer attention.

The important question is whether the child is breathing comfortably and sleeping well.

## What Are the Signs of Mouth Breathing in Children?

Common signs of mouth breathing in children include an open-mouth resting posture, dry lips, snoring, noisy breathing and regularly sleeping with the mouth open.

Some children also experience disturbed sleep or daytime tiredness.

However, these symptoms can have several different causes.

## What Causes Mouth Breathing in Children?

The most common reasons for persistent mouth breathing include nasal congestion, allergies, enlarged adenoids or tonsils and anatomical differences affecting the nasal airway.

Some children may also develop an established open-mouth breathing pattern.

### 1. Allergies and hay fever

Allergic rhinitis can cause swelling inside the nose.

Children may experience congestion, sneezing, an itchy nose or watery eyes.

When nasal passages become congested, breathing through the mouth may feel easier.

Allergic symptoms can vary with seasons, household allergens and environmental exposure.

If you suspect allergies, your child's GP can help determine whether further assessment or treatment is appropriate.

### 2. Enlarged adenoids

Adenoids are tissues located behind the nose in the upper throat.

They form part of the body's immune system.

In some children, enlarged adenoids can restrict airflow through the nose.

This may contribute to mouth breathing, snoring or disturbed sleep.

Adenoids cannot generally be assessed simply by looking inside the child's mouth.

Medical assessment, sometimes involving an ENT specialist, may be required.

### 3. Enlarged tonsils

Tonsils are located towards the back of the throat.

When enlarged, they can narrow the upper airway.

Some children with enlarged tonsils snore or experience breathing difficulties during sleep.

However, tonsil size alone does not determine whether a child has obstructive sleep apnoea.

The diagnosis requires assessment of the symptoms and, when indicated, further testing.

### 4. Frequent colds and nasal congestion

Young children commonly experience respiratory infections.

During a cold, nasal congestion can make nose breathing more difficult.

Temporary mouth breathing often improves as the illness resolves.

However, if the child continues mouth breathing after the congestion has cleared, further assessment may be useful.

### 5. Nasal anatomical differences

Some children have anatomical differences that affect nasal airflow.

These may include a deviated nasal septum or other structural concerns.

Such problems require medical examination.

Dental exercises cannot correct every anatomical obstruction.

### 6. Habitual open-mouth posture

Some children continue resting with their lips apart even when nasal breathing is possible.

This may involve oral muscle habits, tongue position or other functional patterns.

However, it is important to rule out nasal obstruction before assuming the problem is purely behavioural.

We do not recommend encouraging a child to keep their mouth closed if they are struggling to breathe comfortably through their nose.

## Why Does My Child Breathe Through Their Mouth While Sleeping?

Mouth breathing while sleeping can occur when nasal airflow is restricted or when the child has an established open-mouth posture.

However, persistent nighttime mouth breathing may also occur alongside sleep-disordered breathing.

### Is mouth breathing at night always a problem?

No.

Occasional nighttime mouth breathing without other symptoms may not indicate a significant problem.

However, repeated mouth breathing associated with snoring, gasping, disrupted sleep or daytime tiredness should be assessed.

We recommend observing the overall sleep pattern rather than focusing on the child's lip position alone.

### Why does my child sleep with their mouth open?

Possible explanations include nasal congestion, enlarged adenoids or tonsils, allergies and habitual open-mouth posture.

The cause cannot be confirmed by appearance alone.

A GP or other appropriate medical practitioner can help investigate persistent breathing concerns.

### Can mouth breathing affect sleep quality?

It can be associated with conditions that disrupt sleep.

For example, nasal obstruction and childhood obstructive sleep apnoea may occur alongside persistent mouth breathing.

Sleep disruption can affect a child's daytime energy and wellbeing.

However, mouth breathing alone does not prove that sleep apnoea is present.

## What Is the Connection Between Mouth Breathing and Snoring?

Snoring happens when tissues within the upper airway vibrate during breathing.

Some children snore occasionally, particularly during respiratory infections.

Others snore regularly because of airway narrowing.

### Is snoring normal in children?

Snoring is relatively common in childhood.

However, persistent or loud snoring should not automatically be dismissed, particularly when it is associated with breathing pauses, gasping or restless sleep.

The Australian parenting resource [ Raising Children Network explains childhood snoring](https://raisingchildren.net.au/guides/a-z-health-reference/snoring), including the relationship between nasal obstruction, enlarged tonsils and sleep-disordered breathing.

### What is obstructive sleep apnoea in children?

Obstructive sleep apnoea, or OSA, is a condition in which the upper airway repeatedly narrows or becomes blocked during sleep.

This can interfere with breathing and sleep quality.

Possible symptoms include:

- Loud or persistent snoring
- Pauses in breathing
- Gasping or choking during sleep
- Restless sleep
- Unusual sleeping positions
- Morning headaches
- Daytime tiredness
- Difficulty with attention or concentration

However, not every child who snores has OSA.

A medical assessment is required to determine whether sleep-disordered breathing is present.

### When should you see a doctor about snoring?

We recommend speaking with your child's GP if snoring is frequent, loud or accompanied by other concerning symptoms.

A GP may recommend further assessment by a paediatrician, ENT specialist or sleep physician.

The need for a sleep study depends on the clinical findings.

## Can Mouth Breathing Affect a Child's Teeth and Jaw Development?

Persistent mouth breathing has been associated with certain patterns of dental alignment, jaw development and oral posture.

However, the relationship is complex.

Mouth breathing does not inevitably cause crooked teeth or an altered facial shape.

Genetics, growth, oral habits, nasal obstruction and other factors may all contribute.

### Can mouth breathing cause crowded teeth?

Some studies have found associations between habitual mouth breathing and dental crowding or narrower upper dental arches.

However, this does not mean that mouth breathing alone causes every case of crowding.

Children may develop crowded teeth for several reasons, including inherited jaw and tooth-size relationships.

We assess the child's dental development and breathing history together rather than assuming one causes the other.

### Can mouth breathing affect the upper jaw?

Some children who mouth breathe also have a narrow upper palate or particular jaw growth patterns.

However, it can be difficult to separate the effects of breathing patterns from genetics and other developmental influences.

A dental assessment can identify whether the upper jaw and teeth are developing within expected patterns.

Not every narrow palate requires early treatment.

### Does mouth breathing change facial appearance?

Research has identified associations between persistent mouth breathing and certain facial growth characteristics in some groups of children.

However, individual facial development is influenced by many factors.

It would be inaccurate to suggest that every child who mouth breathes will develop a particular facial appearance.

We focus on clinically meaningful findings rather than making predictions about how a child's face will look.

### What about tongue posture?

The tongue normally contributes to oral function, including speaking, swallowing and resting posture.

Some children who mouth breathe also rest their tongue lower in the mouth.

This may be relevant to dental development or oral muscle function.

However, tongue posture alone does not diagnose an airway disorder.

At Smile Star Dental, we assess tongue position alongside the child's bite, breathing history and overall oral function.

## Can Mouth Breathing Increase the Risk of Tooth Decay?

Mouth breathing can contribute to oral dryness.

Saliva helps protect teeth by buffering acids, supporting remineralisation and washing away food particles.

When the mouth is frequently dry, some of these protective effects may be reduced.

### Why does mouth breathing cause dry mouth?

When a child regularly breathes through the mouth, airflow can increase moisture loss from oral surfaces.

Some children wake with dry lips, a dry mouth or an uncomfortable throat.

However, dry mouth can also occur because of medicines, dehydration or other health conditions.

### Does dry mouth cause cavities?

Reduced saliva can increase susceptibility to tooth decay in some circumstances.

However, cavities develop through several interacting factors, including bacterial plaque, dietary sugar exposure and oral hygiene.

Mouth breathing should not be treated as the sole explanation for decay.

We recommend maintaining regular brushing, fluoride use and dental examinations.

### Can mouth breathing cause bad breath?

Dry mouth may contribute to unpleasant breath.

However, bad breath can also arise from plaque accumulation, tongue coating, gum inflammation and other conditions.

A dental examination can help identify oral contributors.

## Can Mouth Breathing Affect Behaviour or Concentration?

Children with disrupted sleep may experience tiredness, irritability or difficulty concentrating.

However, mouth breathing itself does not establish the cause of behavioural concerns.

### Why might poor sleep affect attention?

Adequate sleep supports learning, mood and normal daytime functioning.

When a child experiences repeated sleep disruption, they may struggle with concentration or emotional regulation.

Some children become sleepy, while others appear unusually active.

### Does mouth breathing cause ADHD?

There is no basis for diagnosing attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder from a mouth breathing habit.

Sleep-disordered breathing can sometimes produce symptoms that overlap with attention or behavioural difficulties.

However, ADHD has multiple possible contributing factors and requires appropriate clinical assessment.

We recommend discussing persistent behavioural or learning concerns with your child's GP or paediatrician.

### Should you investigate breathing if your child is always tired?

Yes, especially if tiredness occurs alongside snoring, gasping or restless sleep.

However, fatigue can have many causes.

A medical assessment can help identify whether sleep or breathing problems are contributing.

## How Do You Know if Your Child Needs an Airway Assessment?

An assessment may be appropriate when mouth breathing is persistent or occurs alongside other concerns.

You do not need to wait until your child has significant dental crowding or sleep difficulties to raise the question.

### Signs worth discussing with a healthcare professional

- Regular mouth breathing while awake
- Frequent mouth breathing during sleep
- Persistent snoring
- Pauses or gasping during sleep
- Ongoing nasal congestion
- Restless sleep
- Unexplained daytime tiredness
- Difficulty keeping lips together at rest
- Concerns about dental crowding or jaw development
- Persistent swallowing or speech concerns

Some children will require medical investigation.

Others may benefit from dental monitoring or oral function assessment.

### When is mouth breathing an emergency?

Seek urgent medical help if your child has severe breathing difficulty, blue or grey lips, significant respiratory distress, or prolonged breathing pauses with distress.

For ongoing but non-emergency symptoms, arrange an assessment with your child's GP.

Dental appointments should not delay necessary medical care.

## What Treatment Options Are Available for Mouth Breathing in Children?

Treatment depends on the underlying cause.

There is no single mouth breathing treatment that is appropriate for every child.

### Treatment for allergies or nasal congestion

When allergies or nasal inflammation affect breathing, medical treatment may be recommended.

A GP may discuss appropriate allergy management or refer the child for further assessment.

Medication should be used according to medical advice.

Dental exercises cannot reliably resolve untreated nasal obstruction.

### Treatment for enlarged tonsils or adenoids

If enlarged tonsils or adenoids are contributing to airway problems, an ENT specialist may assess whether treatment is required.

Some children may be monitored or receive medical management.

Others may be considered for surgery when clinically indicated.

The appropriate approach depends on the child's condition and symptoms.

### Myofunctional therapy

Orofacial myofunctional therapy involves exercises intended to improve specific tongue, lip and swallowing functions.

At Smile Star Dental, we offer [ myofunctional therapy](https://smilestardental.com.au/services/myofunctional-therapy/) for selected patients.

However, these exercises should not be presented as a cure for every case of mouth breathing.

They do not remove enlarged adenoids, correct all nasal obstructions or independently treat obstructive sleep apnoea.

### Orthodontic assessment

Some children with significant dental or jaw discrepancies may benefit from orthodontic assessment.

Treatment may involve monitoring, an appliance or another orthodontic approach.

However, appliances should not be prescribed solely on the assumption that a wider jaw will resolve every breathing concern.

Dental alignment and medical airway treatment are related but distinct considerations.

### Monitoring without immediate intervention

Some children do not require active dental treatment.

If the findings are mild and there are no concerning sleep or medical symptoms, monitoring may be appropriate.

We explain what to watch for and when reassessment may be useful.

## How Does Myofunctional Therapy Help Children Who Mouth Breathe?

Myofunctional therapy focuses on the function of muscles involved in tongue posture, lip closure and swallowing.

For selected children, this may include exercises intended to encourage comfortable nasal breathing once medical causes of obstruction have been addressed.

### What happens during therapy?

A program may involve:

- Assessing tongue mobility and resting position
- Reviewing lip closure
- Practising appropriate swallowing patterns
- Teaching selected oral muscle exercises
- Supporting consistent home practice
- Reviewing progress across appointments

The program is tailored to the child's age, abilities and clinical needs.

### Does myofunctional therapy work for every child?

No.

Its benefits depend on the condition being treated, the child's ability to participate and other factors.

Evidence for different applications is still developing.

It should not be promoted as a guaranteed way to prevent crooked teeth or cure childhood sleep apnoea.

### How long does therapy take?

Some programs involve several appointments over weeks or months.

The duration depends on the goals and the child's progress.

Home exercises may be an important part of the program.

However, not every child requires a lengthy course of treatment.

### Can therapy replace an ENT appointment?

No.

When a child has suspected nasal obstruction, enlarged adenoids or possible sleep apnoea, medical assessment is important.

Myofunctional therapy may be considered alongside other care, but it is not a substitute for diagnosis and treatment of an airway condition.

## Can Mouth Breathing in Children Be Corrected Naturally?

Some cases improve when a temporary cause resolves.

For example, mouth breathing associated with a cold may settle as nasal congestion improves.

However, persistent mouth breathing should not be managed through home exercises alone without considering the cause.

### What can parents do at home?

Parents can observe their child's breathing patterns and support general health habits.

Helpful steps may include:

- Noting whether mouth breathing occurs during the day or night
- Monitoring snoring and sleep quality
- Supporting good oral hygiene
- Discussing ongoing nasal congestion with a GP
- Maintaining an age-appropriate sleep routine
- Following professional advice for known allergies

These steps can help identify patterns and support appropriate care.

### Should you tell your child to keep their mouth closed?

Not if they appear to have difficulty breathing through their nose.

Mouth breathing may be compensating for a blocked nasal airway.

Forcing the lips closed does not address the obstruction.

We recommend assessing why nasal breathing is difficult before attempting to change the child's breathing pattern.

### Is mouth taping safe for children?

We do not recommend mouth taping as a home treatment for children.

It may be particularly unsafe when nasal airflow is restricted or an undiagnosed sleep-related breathing condition is present.

A child who is mouth breathing because they cannot breathe adequately through the nose needs appropriate assessment, not forced mouth closure.

### Can breathing exercises replace medical treatment?

No.

Exercises may be useful in selected cases after appropriate assessment.

However, they cannot reliably correct enlarged tonsils, structural nasal blockage or another medical condition requiring treatment.

### Can a dentist diagnose sleep apnoea in children?

A dentist can identify symptoms or oral findings that raise concern about sleep-disordered breathing.

However, a dental examination cannot establish a diagnosis of paediatric obstructive sleep apnoea.

Medical assessment and, when appropriate, sleep testing are needed.

The [ Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne's guide to childhood obstructive sleep apnoea](https://www.rch.org.au/kidsinfo/fact_sheets/childhood_obstructive_sleep_apnoea_osa/) outlines symptoms including snoring, breathing pauses, gasping, restless sleep and mouth breathing at night.

### When would we recommend an ENT referral?

An ENT referral may be considered when there are concerns about enlarged tonsils or adenoids, persistent nasal obstruction or other upper airway issues.

Our role is to recognise relevant findings and help families understand when medical input may be appropriate.

## At What Age Should You Address Mouth Breathing?

There is no single age when every child should begin treatment.

Assessment can be appropriate whenever persistent symptoms are present.

However, the treatment recommended depends on the child's developmental stage and the underlying condition.

### Mouth breathing in toddlers

Toddlers may breathe through their mouths during respiratory infections.

However, persistent snoring, sleep disturbance or breathing difficulty deserves medical assessment.

A GP or paediatrician can help investigate possible causes.

### Mouth breathing in preschool children

Preschool-aged children may have enlarged tonsils or adenoids that contribute to noisy breathing and sleep symptoms.

They may also develop oral habits affecting lip posture or swallowing.

Assessment should focus first on the child's health and airway.

### Mouth breathing in school-aged children

School-aged children may show more noticeable dental crowding or bite changes.

This can be an appropriate time to assess dental development alongside any breathing concerns.

However, treatment is based on individual findings rather than age alone.

### Mouth breathing in teenagers

Teenagers may continue to experience nasal obstruction, allergies or sleep-related breathing problems.

Orthodontic and oral function concerns may also be relevant.

A coordinated approach remains important.

## Quick Answers: Mouth Breathing in Children FAQs

### Is mouth breathing in children normal?

Occasional mouth breathing during a cold or nasal congestion is common. Persistent mouth breathing, particularly with snoring or disturbed sleep, should be assessed.

### Why does my child breathe through their mouth while sleeping?

Possible causes include nasal congestion, allergies, enlarged tonsils or adenoids, and established breathing habits. A medical assessment may be needed if the pattern persists or sleep symptoms are present.

### Can mouth breathing cause crooked teeth?

Persistent mouth breathing is associated with certain dental and jaw development patterns, but it does not inevitably cause crooked teeth. Genetics, growth and other factors also influence alignment.

### Can myofunctional therapy help a child who mouth breathes?

It may help selected children improve oral muscle and breathing-related habits after appropriate assessment. It does not replace medical treatment for nasal obstruction or sleep apnoea.

### When should I worry about my child's mouth breathing?

Seek assessment if mouth breathing is frequent and accompanied by snoring, gasping, breathing pauses, restless sleep or daytime tiredness. Severe breathing difficulty requires emergency medical care.
